What Is a Capability Statement?

It is the one-page document government buyers ask for when they meet a new vendor — part resume, part menu, all business. Here is what goes on it, section by section, and what makes buyers set one aside.

The short answer

A capability statement is a one-page summary of your company written for government buyers. It answers the five questions a contracting officer, a small business coordinator, or a prime contractor has when they first encounter you: What do you do? Who have you done it for? Why you over the next company? How do I verify you? How do I reach you? You will hand it over at industry days and pre-bid meetings, attach it to sources sought responses (see our guide on finding your first government contract), and send it as the follow-up to almost every first conversation in this market. It assumes your SAM.gov registration is done, because the identification numbers it carries come from there.

The five sections, and what belongs in each

1. Header and contact block.
Company legal name, logo if you have one, a one-line description of what you do, and a named contact with a direct phone and email. Example: "Licensed home-care and staffing services across Broward County — Jane Doe, Owner, (555) 010-2200." A generic inbox and no named person is a quiet way to lose follow-ups.

2. Core competencies.
A short bullet list of the specific services you sell, written in the buyer's vocabulary. Strong example: "Nightly janitorial service for office buildings; floor care and restroom sanitation; post-construction cleanup." Weak example: "All your cleaning needs!" Name the service, the setting, and any scale you can truthfully state.

3. Past performance.
Three to five short entries naming the customer type, the work, and when you did it. Government work goes first if you have it — but commercial work counts. Example: "Recurring office cleaning for a regional property management firm, 12 locations, since 2023." Describe only work you actually performed; buyers verify.

4. Differentiators.
Two or three concrete reasons to pick you: coverage area, response times you genuinely meet, licenses and insurance you actually carry, bilingual staff, specialized equipment, owner-operated supervision. A differentiator is a fact, not an adjective — "fully licensed and insured in Florida" informs; "world-class excellence" does not.

5. Company data.
The verification block: UEI, CAGE code, your primary NAICS codes (a short list, not every code you registered), business type, and any certifications you hold — 8(a), HUBZone, women-owned, service-disabled veteran-owned, or state certifications. This is the section a buyer uses to look you up, so every number must be current and correct.

Make more than one version

One generic page is the starting point, not the finish. Keep a master version, then tailor copies for the buyers you actually pursue: a version for a federal agency emphasizes your federal registrations and NAICS codes; a version for a prime contractor emphasizes the exact services you can subcontract and your capacity. Tailoring means reordering competencies and past performance so the most relevant items sit at the top — it never means inventing experience for the audience. Save the result as a clean PDF with your company name in the file name, and keep an editable copy so updating it takes minutes, because you should update it every time your facts change.

Mistakes that make buyers set the page aside

  • Too long. A capability statement is one page. Four pages of company history is a brochure, and it will be skimmed like one — briefly.
  • All adjectives, no facts. "Leading provider of innovative solutions" tells a buyer nothing they can act on. Specific services, settings, and dates do.
  • Claiming work you have not done. Past performance is checked — through references, award records, and simple questions you will not answer well. Honesty is not only ethical here; it is survivable.
  • Every NAICS code you ever considered. A page claiming twenty unrelated industries reads as a company that does none of them well. Lead with the few codes that match the page's audience.
  • Stale data. An expired certification, an old phone number, or a UEI that does not match your registration undermines the whole page. Review it whenever your registrations renew.
  • No clear contact. If a busy buyer cannot tell in five seconds who to call, the page has failed at its only job.

For free feedback on your draft, your local APEX Accelerator will typically review capability statements as part of its no-cost counseling, and the SBA publishes guidance for small business sellers. A second pair of experienced eyes is worth having before the page meets a buyer.

Questions

How long should a capability statement be?

One page. A single page — occasionally front and back — that a buyer can scan in under a minute. If it runs longer, it stops being a capability statement and becomes a brochure.

Is a capability statement the same as a brochure or resume?

No. A brochure markets to customers generally; a resume is about a person. A capability statement is structured for government buyers: core competencies, relevant past performance, differentiators, and the registration data (UEI, CAGE, NAICS codes, certifications) a buyer needs to act on.

What if I have no government past performance yet?

Use your commercial and private-sector work, described in terms a buyer cares about — the service, the setting, and the scale. Subcontracting work counts too. Never invent government clients or contracts you do not have.

When do I actually use a capability statement?

At industry days and pre-bid meetings, in introductions to contracting staff and small business coordinators, attached to sources sought responses, and as the follow-up document after a first conversation with a buyer or a prime contractor.
