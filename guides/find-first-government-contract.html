How to Find Your First Government Contract

Governments buy almost everything private customers buy — cleaning, catering, repairs, IT help, supplies, training. The work is posted in public, for free. Finding your first contract is mostly about searching in the right places, in the right way.

Start with what you already sell

Government buyers do not search for companies the way customers search Google. They describe work using NAICS codes (industry codes for what a business does) and plain keywords in their notices. Before you search, write down the two or three services or products you already deliver well, find the NAICS codes that match them, and note the words a buyer would use — "janitorial services," "office furniture," "forklift rental." Your searches will use exactly those codes and words. If you have not registered yet, our SAM.gov registration guide covers the setup; you can search opportunities before your registration is finished, but you generally need an active registration before a federal award can be made to you.

Where opportunities are actually posted

  • Federal contracts: posted on SAM.gov under Contract Opportunities. This is the single official source for federal solicitations above the smallest purchases.
  • Federal grants: posted on Grants.gov — a different kind of opportunity, for programs rather than purchases.
  • State contracts: posted on each state's vendor portal. Florida businesses register and watch solicitations in MyFloridaMarketPlace — see our Florida vendor registration guide.
  • Counties, cities, school districts: each runs its own procurement page or portal. The buyers closest to you often post smaller, simpler work that suits a first contract.

How to search SAM.gov like a buyer

1. Filter by NAICS code.
Enter your primary NAICS code first. This cuts thousands of unrelated notices down to the work in your industry.

2. Add keywords.
Layer in the plain words buyers use for your service. Try a couple of variations — agencies describe the same work differently.

3. Filter by place of performance.
Start with work in your state or within a distance you can genuinely serve. A contract you cannot staff is not an opportunity.

4. Check the set-aside.
Many notices are reserved for categories of small businesses — small business, 8(a), HUBZone, women-owned, service-disabled veteran-owned. If a notice is set aside for a category you hold, your competition is only firms in that category.

5. Watch the dates.
Note the response deadline and any questions deadline. Questions are usually due well before bids are — miss that window and ambiguities stay ambiguous.

6. Save your search and repeat it.
New notices post every business day. The businesses that find good fits search on a rhythm, not once.

Read the notice types correctly

Not every posting is a bid request. A solicitation (often an RFQ for quotes or an RFP for proposals) is an actual invitation to bid — read it fully, including attachments, before deciding. A sources sought notice is earlier: the agency is asking which companies could do upcoming work. Responding to sources sought costs little, puts your name in front of the buyer, and can influence whether the requirement is set aside for small businesses. A pre-solicitation notice is a heads-up that a solicitation is coming. Treat sources sought responses as marketing with a deadline: answer what is asked, attach your capability statement, and submit on time.

Picking a first bid you can actually win and deliver

  • Match your real work. Bid on what you already do for private customers. A first contract is the wrong place to learn a new trade.
  • Keep it small and simple. Smaller requirements with clear specifications are more realistic first wins than large, multi-site awards — and delivering well builds the past performance record that bigger bids ask about.
  • Price it honestly. Work out your true cost — labor, materials, travel, insurance, and the cost of waiting for government payment terms — before quoting. A won contract that loses money is worse than a lost bid.
  • Follow the instructions exactly. Many bids are rejected for format, signature, or deadline failures, not price. Responsive beats clever.
  • Consider subcontracting. Working under an experienced prime contractor is a legitimate first step: real government work, real past performance, less paperwork.

Finally, get the free help that exists for exactly this stage. APEX Accelerators provide no-cost counseling on finding and bidding government work, and the SBA runs the certification programs behind the set-aside categories. Nobody has to do this alone, and no honest guide should promise you a win — what you control is showing up prepared, on the right notices, again and again.

Questions

Where are government contracts posted?

Federal contract opportunities are posted on SAM.gov. State work is posted on state vendor portals — in Florida, MyFloridaMarketPlace — and counties, cities, and school districts run their own procurement pages and portals.

Can I search for contracts before I am registered?

Yes. Searching SAM.gov opportunities is free and does not require a completed registration. You generally do need an active SAM.gov registration before you can be awarded a federal contract.

What is a set-aside?

A set-aside reserves a contract for a category of small businesses — for example small businesses generally, or 8(a), HUBZone, women-owned, or service-disabled veteran-owned firms. If a notice is set aside for a category you are certified in, only firms in that category compete for it.

Should my first government bid be a big contract?

Usually not. A smaller, simpler requirement that matches work you already do is a more realistic first win, and delivering it well builds the past performance that larger bids ask for.
