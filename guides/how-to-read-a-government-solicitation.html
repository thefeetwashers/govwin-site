How to Read a Government Solicitation, Section by Section

A federal solicitation looks like a wall of legal language — but it follows the same map every time. Learn what sections A through M tell you, and you can size up any opportunity in about an hour.

First, find the clock

Before you read a single requirement, find the dates. Section A is the cover page — usually the form SF-1449 — and it holds the solicitation number, the issuing office, the point of contact, and the date and time offers are due. Write them down first: a proposal that arrives late is typically set aside without being evaluated. Then check Section F for the period of performance — when work starts, how long it runs, and whether the agency can extend it with option years. Finally, look in Section L for the questions deadline. It falls before the response deadline, and it is your only window to get an unclear requirement explained in writing. If the due date has passed, or the performance period collides with work you cannot move, stop here.

What they are buying — and how you get paid

Section B is the price schedule: each item or service the agency wants, the quantity, the unit, and the space where you enter your price. Read it line by line: every line is something you must price, and a missing line can mean a rejected bid. Note whether the contract is fixed-price — you carry the cost risk — or time-and-materials, where the agency pays for the hours you work. Section C is the description of the work itself — the specifications for goods, or the statement of work for services. This is the heart of the solicitation: tasks, deliverables, quality standards, and reports the agency expects. Mark every requirement, every deliverable, and anything you cannot do at a price the work will bear. If Section C describes work outside your capability, that is a no-bid decision — making it early saves days you would have spent on a proposal you could not deliver.

The rules that come with the work

Sections D through I are the contract terms you would live under if you won — the driest pages, and still worth a careful pass. Section D covers packaging and marking for goods. Section E covers inspection and acceptance: who checks the work, and what happens if it is rejected. Section G covers contract administration: your contracting officer, invoice submission, and payment. Section H holds special requirements unique to this buy — security clearances, key-personnel rules, or subcontracting limits. Section I lists the contract clauses, many incorporated by reference to the Federal Acquisition Regulation. You need not memorize every clause — just spot the ones that change your cost or risk — clauses on termination, subcontracting limits, and labor standards. If a clause would force you to change how you run your business, price it in or walk away.

Attachments are not optional reading

Section J lists the attachments, and this is where bidders most often get caught. Attachments can include the performance work statement, pricing templates, past-performance questionnaires, wage determinations, drawings, and forms you must return with your proposal. A pricing template is part of the bid, not a suggestion — use it if the solicitation says so. Download every attachment, check none is corrupted or missing, and note anything referenced that is not attached. If a referenced document is missing, ask the point of contact before the questions deadline — do not guess at its contents.

Sections K and L: the paperwork that gets you in the door

Section K holds representations and certifications — formal statements about your size status, ownership, and whether you have been debarred. Much of this lives in your SAM.gov registration, which is free, so keep it current and accurate; a stale record can make a true statement in Section K false. If you have not registered yet, start with our SAM.gov registration guide. Section L is the instructions to offerors — how to build and submit your proposal. It tells you the page limits, the format, how many volumes to submit, what goes in each, and how files are delivered. Treat Section L as a checklist: every instruction is a test, and missing one is a common reason a strong proposal is rejected unscored. Read it twice — when you decide to bid, and the day before you submit.

Section M: how they pick the winner

Section M is the evaluation criteria, and it is the section experienced bidders read first, right after the dates. It states the factors the agency scores — typically technical approach, past performance, and price — and how much each matters. Read it for the order of importance: if technical approach outranks price, the lowest bid does not win, and a proposal that undercuts on price while skimping on the technical story will lose. Note whether factors are in descending order of importance, or given weights. Then build your proposal to the factors: answer each one directly, in the order given, with evidence, and do not bury your best proof where nobody scores it. A proposal that mirrors Section M is easy to score — and easy to score is easy to score well.

A first-pass routine that takes about an hour

  • Find the dates: response deadline, questions deadline, and period of performance — Sections A, F, and L.
  • Check the fit. Read Section C and the set-aside notice in Section A. If the work or eligibility does not fit, stop.
  • Price the shape of it. Scan Section B and the Section I clauses that change your cost. If you cannot see a way to price it profitably, stop.
  • Read Section M. Understand what wins, in what order, before you write a word.
  • Collect the attachments. Download everything in Section J and confirm nothing is missing.
  • Decide go or no-go — in writing. A short note with your reasons makes the next one faster, whether this is a bid or a pass.

This guide explains how solicitations are organized; it is general information, not legal advice. Procurement rules and protest procedures are specific to each solicitation and agency, so if a requirement or deadline could affect your rights, confirm it in the solicitation itself and consider speaking with a procurement attorney before you act.

Questions

Do all federal solicitations use sections A through M?

Most do. The A-through-M layout is the Uniform Contract Format used for negotiated procurements and many sealed bids. Some solicitations — simplified acquisitions, requests for quotations, and task orders under existing contracts — use shorter formats, but the same kinds of information still appear somewhere in the document.

What is the most common reason a bid is rejected without being read?

Missing the deadline or the submission instructions. A proposal that arrives late, in the wrong format, or to the wrong place is typically set aside without evaluation, however strong it is. That is why the first thing to find is the response date and time in Section A and the exact instructions in Section L.

If a solicitation is amended, do I have to start over?

No, but read the amendment carefully. Amendments can change the deadline, the scope of work, the pricing schedule, or the instructions. When an amendment is issued, acknowledge it exactly as told — failing to acknowledge an amendment is a common reason a bid is rejected.

Can I ask the agency a question about a solicitation?

Yes — whenever something is unclear. The solicitation names a point of contact and a questions deadline, usually in Section A or Section L. Ask in writing before the questions deadline, and watch for the agency's answers, normally shared with every bidder through an amendment.
